Black-Scholes: the formula and what drives it

Make the tree steps infinitely small and you get one closed formula. Five inputs, one price.

The idea

The copying argument from step 2 survives when trading becomes continuous. At every instant, a position of Δ shares against one option has no exposure to small price changes, so it must earn the risk-free rate. That single requirement leads to a formula for the option price.

The model also needs a description of how the price moves. It assumes the percentage change each instant is random with a constant size, which makes the price at expiry lognormal: always positive, and skewed to the right.

Terms in one breath

Volatility, σ
The yearly size of random price moves, as a percentage. 25% means a typical yearly move of about a quarter.
Risk-free rate, r
The interest rate for money with no risk. Used to discount and to grow the cash in the copy.
Dividend yield, q
Yearly payout of the underlying as a share of its price. Paying out lowers the stock, so it lowers call prices.
Forward price
S·e(r − q)T. The price you would agree today to trade at expiry.
N(x)
The chance that a standard bell-curve variable is below x. It turns d1 and d2 into probabilities.
Lognormal
The log of the price is bell-shaped. Prices stay above zero and the upper tail is longer.
Put-call parity
Call − put = S·e−qT − K·e−rT. It holds for any model with no arbitrage.

The formulas

d1 = [ ln(S/K) + (r − q + σ²/2)·T ] / (σ√T)
d2 = d1 − σ√T
Call = S·e−qT·N(d1) − K·e−rT·N(d2)
Put = K·e−rT·N(−d2) − S·e−qT·N(−d1)

Read the call formula as a story. The first term is the value today of receiving the asset if the option finishes in the money. The second is the value today of paying the strike in that case. N(d2) is the risk-neutral chance of finishing in the money.

Lab 5: the price

Try this: raise the volatility and watch both the call and the put get dearer. Raise the rate: calls rise, puts fall. Shorten the time: the curve collapses onto the payoff.

Lab 6: the distribution behind the price

The price is a discounted average of the payoff over the lognormal distribution. Doing that integral numerically gives the same number as the formula.

Lab 7: the price surface

What the model assumes

Constant volatility
Fails: the market charges different volatilities for different strikes. Step 5.
Smooth prices
Fails: real prices jump, for example on news or in a crash.
Continuous hedging
You can only trade at discrete times, and each trade costs money.
Constant rate and dividend
Both change over time. Desks use full curves instead.
Go deeper: two routes to the formula

Route 1, the hedge. Let V(S, t) be the option value. Hold the option and short Δ = ∂V/∂S shares. The random part of the position cancels, so the position must grow at the risk-free rate. This gives the Black-Scholes equation:

∂V/∂t + ½σ²S²·∂²V/∂S² + (r − q)·S·∂V/∂S − r·V = 0

It is solved backwards from the known payoff at expiry. Step 4 uses this equation to relate theta and gamma.

Route 2, the average. Price = e−rT × the average of the payoff when the stock grows at the rate r − q on average. With a lognormal price this integral gives N(d1) and N(d2). Lab 6 does it by brute force. Step 6 does it by simulation.
