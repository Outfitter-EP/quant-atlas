The Greeks: how an option reacts

Each Greek is the slope of the price against one input. Together they tell you what a position will do tomorrow.

The idea

An option price depends on spot, time, volatility and the rate. A trader holding options wants to know how much money is lost or made when each input moves a little. The answers are the Greeks. Banks limit and hedge them every day.

Terms in one breath

Delta, Δ
Change in option value for a $1 move in spot. Also the number of shares that hedges one option. A call is between 0 and 1, a put between −1 and 0.
Gamma, Γ
Change in delta for a $1 move in spot. High gamma means the hedge goes stale fast. Largest at the money close to expiry.
Vega, ν
Change in value for one point of volatility. Long options means long vega: you gain when volatility rises.
Theta, Θ
Change in value as one day passes. Long options lose value with time, short options earn it.
Rho, ρ
Change in value for a 1% move in the interest rate. Usually the smallest effect for short options.
Delta hedge
Trade shares so that total delta is zero. The position is then flat for small moves in spot.
P&L explain
Splitting a day's profit into delta, gamma, vega and theta pieces plus what is left over.

The formulas

Δcall = e−qT·N(d1),   Δput = Δcall − e−qT
Γ = e−qT·φ(d1) / (S·σ√T)
ν = S·e−qT·φ(d1)·√T
Θcall = −S·e−qT·φ(d1)·σ / (2√T) − r·K·e−rT·N(d2) + q·S·e−qT·N(d1)
ρcall = K·T·e−rT·N(d2)  φ is the bell-curve height

Lab 9: what moved my option?

After a day of trading, a risk team asks why the position changed value. The answer is the Taylor expansion of the price: delta times the spot move, plus half gamma times the move squared, plus vega times the volatility change, plus theta times the time that passed.

Try this: make the spot move large. The delta-only line misses the curve and the parabola catches most of it. For small moves the error is tiny, which is why hedgers rebalance often.

Go deeper: gamma and theta are two sides of one trade

Put the Greeks into the Black-Scholes equation from step 3 and it reads Θ + ½σ²S²Γ + (r − q)SΔ − rV = 0. For a delta-hedged position the delta and rate terms nearly cancel, leaving Θ ≈ −½σ²S²Γ.

The meaning: if you own gamma you pay theta every day, and if you sell gamma you collect it. A long option position that is hedged makes money when the stock moves more than the volatility used to price it, and loses the daily decay when it moves less.
