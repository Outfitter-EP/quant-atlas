Implied volatility and the smile

The market gives prices. Running Black-Scholes backwards turns them into volatilities, and those volatilities are not all the same.

The idea

In Black-Scholes every input is observable except volatility. The market, meanwhile, shows the option price. So traders flip the question: which volatility makes the formula match this price? That number is the implied volatility. Because price rises with volatility at every strike, there is exactly one answer, and desks quote options in volatility instead of price.

If the model were right, every strike and expiry would give the same number. They do not. Low strikes usually carry higher implied volatility than high ones. Plotted against strike, the points form a skew or a smile. Across strikes and maturities they form a volatility surface.

Terms in one breath

Implied volatility
The volatility that makes the model price equal the market price. A quote for the option's price level, not a forecast by itself.
Realised volatility
How much the price actually moved over a past period.
Skew
Implied volatility falling as the strike rises. Typical for equity indices: downside protection is expensive.
Smile
Higher implied volatility on both wings than at the money. Common in currencies.
Term structure
Implied volatility at the money against maturity.
Volatility surface
Implied volatility as a function of strike and maturity.
Newton's method
Root finding by sliding down tangent lines. Here the slope is vega, so it is fast when vega is large and unstable when it is tiny.
Bisection
Root finding by halving an interval that contains the answer. Slow but safe.
Risk-neutral density
The market's implied distribution of the future price, recovered from option prices across strikes.
Butterfly arbitrage
A smile so bumpy that a butterfly spread would have a negative price. The implied density would go below zero.

Why a skew exists

Investors pay up for protection against crashes, and crashes in equities tend to come with volatility spikes. Both push put prices, and so their implied volatilities, above what a lognormal model would give. Skew is also steeper for short maturities, because a crash is a larger part of a short life.

Go deeper: how option prices reveal a distribution

The call price is C(K) = e−rT ∫ (s − K)+ f(s) ds, where f is the risk-neutral density of the price at expiry. Differentiate once with respect to the strike: ∂C/∂K = −e−rT × (chance that the price ends above K). Differentiate again and you get

∂²C/∂K² = e−rT · f(K)

So the second difference of call prices across strikes is the density, which is what the lab computes. Density cannot be negative, so butterfly spreads cannot have a negative price. That is the arbitrage rule a smile must obey. Practitioners fit smooth, arbitrage-free families such as SVI to quotes for exactly this reason.
